Career Strategy

What Our Findings Mean for a Student Targeting the Data Analyst to Data Scientist Pathway

Overview

The earlier pages describe the market, compare it with our team’s skills, and model pay. This page turns those results into a plan for a student or early-career job seeker on the Data Analyst to Data Scientist pathway in NAICS 5182 (Computing Infrastructure Providers, Data Processing, Web Hosting, and Related Services). It answers the last research question of the project: what should a job seeker learn first, and where should they apply?

Everything here comes from the 765 pathway postings in the MET Career Compass 2026 jobs data (see Data Preparation), from the models on the Predictive Modeling page, and from the team skill comparison on the Skill Gap Analysis page. It also feeds the written final report. The patterns are associations in posted jobs, not promises about any one offer; the last section lists the limits.

The short version:

  1. Enter through the analyst toolkit (SQL, visualization, communication) and show it in a portfolio.
  2. Make Machine Learning and Python the bridge to Data Scientist and ML Engineer roles. This is where the largest pay difference is.
  3. Add cloud and data-pipeline skills, which this industry values, and document your experience early.

The Four Roles at a Glance

The pathway has four role families. The table puts the main facts for each in one place, using the same scoring as the Role Opportunity Scorecard (pay 40%, accessibility 30%, fit with our team 30%).

rows = []

How to read it: ML Engineer and Data Scientist score highest because they pay the most (median posted pay of $220,260 and $204,875). Data Analyst is the lowest-paying role (median $114,750) but the most accessible, since 65% of its postings do not use a senior title. That is the trade-off behind the plan below: start where the entry is realistic, then move toward the roles that pay more. Pay medians for Data Analyst rest on only 30 postings with a salary, so treat them as a guide.

A Three-Step Plan

NoteStep 1: Enter through the analyst toolkit

Data Analyst postings most often name communication (35%), SQL (31%), data visualization (21%), Excel (18%), and dashboards (16%). Build one portfolio project that answers a business question with SQL and presents it in a dashboard with a short written summary. Showing that you can communicate results is part of the skill, not an extra.

NoteStep 2: Use Machine Learning and Python as the bridge

Data Scientist and ML Engineer postings name Machine Learning in 68% of cases and Python in 54%, compared with 11% and 20% for Data Analyst postings. Once the role is known, naming Machine Learning adds comparatively little on its own; the bigger gain comes from moving into a role that expects it. Moving from a Data Analyst title to a Data Scientist or ML Engineer title is associated with roughly $72,000 to $82,000 more in disclosed pay, which is larger than any location choice. Complete a supervised-learning project in Python that explains how it was evaluated and what its limits are.

NoteStep 3: Add cloud and pipeline skills, and document your experience

Cloud/AWS appears in 28% of Data Scientist and ML postings and 55% of Data / Analytics Engineer postings, and ETL in 68% of engineer postings. A cloud certification and one analysis run on a cloud platform signal fit with employers in this industry, and Data / Analytics Engineer roles (median posted pay $175,000) are a second route up the pathway. Because the market skews mid-career, document internships, research, and projects in a portfolio early.

Which Skills Should You Learn First?

The chart compares how often each skill appears in Data Analyst postings with how often it appears in the postings for the role you want. Use the dropdown to choose a target role. The skills are sorted by the gap: the skills at the top are the ones the target role asks for much more often than analyst roles do, so they are the ones to learn first.

analyst = tx[tx["role"] == "Data Analyst"]
a_share = analyst[skill_cols].astype(bool).mean()
targets = ["Data Scientist", "ML Engineer", "Data / Analytics Engineer"]
TOP = 8


def view(role):
    t = tx[tx["role"] == role]
    t_share = t[skill_cols].astype(bool).mean()
    gap = (t_share - a_share).sort_values(ascending=False).head(TOP)
    gap = gap[gap > 0.02][::-1]  # only skills the target role names clearly more often
    names = [c[len("skill_"):] for c in gap.index]
    return dict(names=names, analyst=a_share[gap.index].values, target=t_share[gap.index].values,
                gap=gap.values, n=len(t))

Interpretation: the skills that open the way to a Data Scientist or ML Engineer role are Machine Learning, Python, and deep learning, while the Data / Analytics Engineer role leans on cloud, ETL, and data modeling skills. The 68% and 54% quoted in Step 2 pool Data Scientist and ML Engineer postings, while this chart shows each role separately. Skill shares come from searching the posting text, so they undercount real demand and should be compared between roles, not read as exact requirements. Compared with our own team (see Skill Gap Analysis), the clearest group gaps are Machine Learning and Statistics, which is why those come first in our own improvement plan.

Where to Apply

Demand for this pathway is concentrated. Of the 653 postings with a state we could identify, California (141) and New York (113) together hold about 39%. They also show the highest posted pay among the states with enough postings to compare.

Use the Estimator With This Plan

The Predictive Modeling page includes a salary estimator. Use it to test the plan: start with an entry-level analyst profile, then change one thing at a time (the role, the state, a skill such as Machine Learning, or a senior title) and read which changes are associated with higher pay. It is a guide to relative differences, not a prediction of what an employer will offer.

What This Plan Does Not Tell You

  • Associations, not causes. The pay differences describe patterns in posted jobs. They do not show that learning a skill or moving to a state will produce that raise.
  • Small samples. Only 303 postings state a salary, and 30 of them are Data Analyst postings, so close comparisons between roles cannot be separated reliably.
  • Posted pay is not accepted pay. Disclosure is concentrated in California and New York, which makes posted pay look higher than the national median.
  • Text matching. Skills and degrees are matched from posting text, so the shares are lower bounds.
  • Our own judgment. The scorecard weights and the Advanced skill target are team choices, and the team skill ratings are self-reported by three people.
  • One period. The data cover January to September 2026, so the plan cannot show trends over time.

See the Limitations on the Predictive Modeling page for the full list.
